Population Dynamics

How, and why, do populations change in size?
video 0:43–7:10
0:51 Exponential & geometric growth · 1:58 Logistic growth & density dependence · 3:05 Time lags, cycles & chaos · 4:08 Life tables & matrix models · 5:33 Stochasticity, viability & life history

Key equations

Exponential growth
dN/dt = rN,   N(t) = N0ert,   r = b − d,   td = ln 2 / r
Geometric growth
Nt+1 = λNt,   λ = er
Logistic growth
dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K);   MSY = rK/4 at N = K/2
Delayed logistic (Hutchinson)
dN/dt = rN(t)[1 − N(t − τ)/K]: monotonic if rτ < 1/e; damped if 1/e < rτ < π/2; limit cycle if rτ > π/2
Logistic map (May)
xt+1 = rxt(1 − xt): 2-cycle at r = 3, 4-cycle at ≈ 3.449, chaos beyond ≈ 3.57
Life table
R0 = Σ lxmx;   G = Σ x lxmx / R0;   r ≈ ln R0 / G
Matrix model
n(t+1) = A n(t); λ = dominant eigenvalue; w = stable stage distribution; v = reproductive values; sensitivity sij = viwj/⟨v,w⟩; elasticity eij = (aij/λ)sij
Variable environments
λG = (Π λt)1/T ≤ λ̄;   ln λG ≈ ln λ̄ − σ2/(2λ̄2)

No. 0010:52
Intrinsic rate of increase (r)
Instantaneous per-capita growth rate, b − d, under ideal conditions; units of 1/time.
No. 0021:19
Finite rate of increase (λ)
Ratio of population sizes in successive time steps, N(t+1)/N(t); λ = e^r.
No. 0031:41
Density independence
Per-capita birth and death rates that do not change with population density.
No. 0041:59
Density dependence
Per-capita birth or death rates that change with population density; the basis of population regulation.
No. 0052:15
Carrying capacity (K)
Equilibrium density at which per-capita births equal deaths.
No. 0062:15
Maximum sustainable yield
Largest harvest a population can sustain indefinitely; at N = K/2 in the logistic model.
No. 0072:29
Compensation & overcompensation
Recruitment that saturates (Beverton–Holt) versus recruitment that declines at high density (Ricker).
No. 0082:47
Allee effect
Positive density dependence at low density; a strong Allee effect produces an extinction threshold.
No. 0093:06
Time lag
Delay between a change in density and the resulting change in per-capita growth.
No. 0103:16
Limit cycle
A self-sustaining oscillation of fixed amplitude that trajectories approach from any start.
No. 0113:34
Period-doubling bifurcation
Abrupt doubling of a cycle’s period as a parameter increases: 1 → 2 → 4 → … → chaos.
No. 0123:53
Deterministic chaos
Bounded, aperiodic dynamics from a deterministic rule, with sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
No. 0134:09
Survivorship (lₓ)
Proportion of a cohort surviving from birth to age x.
No. 0144:41
Net reproductive rate (R₀)
R₀ = Σ lₓmₓ: expected lifetime offspring per individual; growth per generation.
No. 0154:54
Leslie & Lefkovitch matrices
Projection matrices of age- or stage-specific survival and fecundity; dominant eigenvalue = λ.
No. 0164:54
Stable stage distribution
Constant proportions among stages approached under fixed vital rates (right eigenvector).
No. 0175:13
Elasticity
Proportional change in λ per proportional change in a vital rate; elasticities sum to 1.
No. 0185:34
Demographic stochasticity
Chance variation in individual births and deaths; strongest in small populations.
No. 0195:34
Environmental stochasticity
Temporal variation in vital rates that affects all individuals together; catastrophes are extreme cases.
No. 0205:55
Geometric mean growth
Long-run λ under variability; always ≤ the arithmetic mean, so variance reduces growth.
No. 0216:12
PVA & minimum viable population
Projection of extinction risk; MVP = smallest population with an acceptable persistence probability.
No. 0226:32
Extinction vortex
Mutually reinforcing genetic and demographic declines in small populations (Gilpin & Soulé 1986).
No. 0236:45
r- and K-selection
Life-history strategies favored at low density and high variability (r) versus near carrying capacity (K).

Niche Theory

Where can a species live, and what does it do there?
video 7:10–12:04
7:18 Three ideas of the niche · 8:23 Fundamental & realized niches · 9:23 Partitioning & limiting similarity · 10:50 The modern niche

Key equations

MacArthur–Levins overlap
αij = Σk pikpjk / Σk pik2   (p = proportional use of resource k)
Pianka overlap
Ojk = Σ pijpik / √(Σ pij2 Σ pik2)
Limiting similarity
coexistence requires roughly d/w ≳ 1 (separation of means / utilization width)
Requirement niche (ZNGI)
the set of resource levels where (1/N) dN/dt = f(R1, R2) − m = 0

Competition

What happens when species need the same things?
video 12:04–18:13
12:12 Kinds of competition · 13:52 Lotka–Volterra competition · 15:25 Resource competition & R* · 16:36 Modern coexistence theory

Key equations

Lotka–Volterra competition
dN1/dt = r1N1(K1 − N1 − α12N2)/K1;   dN2/dt = r2N2(K2 − N2 − α21N1)/K2
Outcomes
species 1 wins: K1 > K2/α21 and K1/α12 > K2 · species 2 wins: reverse · stable coexistence: K1 < K2/α21 and K2 < K1/α12 (α12 < K1/K2, α21 < K2/K1) · unstable (priority effects): K1 > K2/α21 and K2 > K1/α12
Resource competition (Tilman)
dN/dt = N[f(R) − m]; R* solves f(R*) = m; with Monod uptake f = μmaxR/(Ks + R), R* = m Ks/(μmax − m)
Modern coexistence (Chesson)
coexistence when ρ < κ1/κ2 < 1/ρ; niche difference = 1 − ρ; fitness ratio = κ1/κ2; invasion criterion: r̄i > 0 when rare

Per-capita effect of one species on another’s growth, relative to intraspecific effects.

No. 04814:50

When arrival order or initial abundance determines the outcome of interactions.
No. 04915:26

No. 05016:19
Supply point
Resource availability in the absence of consumers; locates the outcome on the two-resource plane.
No. 05116:19

Tilman: the ratio of limiting resources supplied determines which species dominate or coexist.
No. 05216:37
Mutual invasibility
Coexistence criterion: each species has a positive growth rate when rare in the other’s presence.
No. 05316:52
Stabilizing mechanism
Niche difference causing stronger intra- than interspecific limitation; favors species when rare.
No. 05416:52

Specialist enemies concentrated near parent trees reduce recruitment of conspecifics nearby.
No. 05617:27

  4. How would you test for apparent competition in a field system?

Predation

How do consumers and their victims shape each other?
video 18:13–24:23
18:19 Predator–prey cycles · 19:32 Functional & numerical responses · 20:49 Optimal foraging · 21:39 Enrichment & stability · 22:49 Keystones, cascades & fear

Key equations

Lotka–Volterra predator–prey
dN/dt = rN − aNP;   dP/dt = caNP − mP;   isoclines N* = m/(ca), P* = r/a
Functional responses (Holling)
Type II: f(N) = aN/(1 + ahN) → 1/h as N → ∞;   Type III: f(N) = aN2/(1 + ahN2)
Rosenzweig–MacArthur
dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) − aNP/(1 + ahN);   dP/dt = caNP/(1 + ahN) − mP
Nicholson–Bailey
Ht+1 = λHte−aPt;   Pt+1 = cHt(1 − e−aPt)
Marginal value theorem
leave when g′(t*) = g(t*)/(τ + t*), the long-term average intake rate including travel time τ
Diet model
add prey type i (ranked by E/h) if Ei/hi > Σj<i λjEj / (1 + Σj<i λjhj)

Key equations

Species–area relationship
S = cAz;   log S = log c + z log A;   islands z ≈ 0.20–0.35, mainland nested samples ≈ 0.12–0.17

Key equations

Levins model
dp/dt = cp(1 − p) − ep;   p* = 1 − e/c; persistence requires c > e
Habitat destruction
dp/dt = cp(1 − D − p) − ep;   p* = 1 − D − e/c; extinction threshold at D = 1 − e/c
Incidence function model (Hanski)
Ji = Ci/(Ci + Ei − CiEi);   Ei = min(1, e/Aix);   Ci = Si2/(Si2 + y2)
Connectivity
Si = Σj≠i pj e−αdij Ajb
Source–sink
sources: λ > 1 (net exporters); sinks: λ < 1 (persist only with immigration)

Rehearsal answers

Why is a Type II functional response destabilizing?

Per-capita predation risk, f(N)/N = a/(1 + ahN), falls as prey density rises, so predation weakens as prey increase: inverse density dependence that amplifies fluctuations.

Why does the species with the lowest R* win?

At equilibrium each consumer holds the resource at its own R*. The species with the lower R* depresses the resource below the other’s break-even level, so the competitor’s growth becomes negative (single limiting resource, well-mixed, equilibrium conditions).

Why don’t empty patches mean habitat is unimportant?

In colonization–extinction dynamics a fraction of suitable patches (e/c in the Levins model) is always empty. Those patches are needed for recolonization; destroying them lowers p* and can cross the extinction threshold.

Stabilizing vs equalizing mechanisms?

Stabilizing mechanisms (niche differences) make intraspecific limitation exceed interspecific limitation and give rare species an advantage. Equalizing mechanisms shrink average fitness differences. Only stabilizing mechanisms create a rare-species advantage.

How do the four paradigms differ?

Patch dynamics: homogeneous patches, competition–colonization trade-off. Species sorting: heterogeneous patches, moderate dispersal, composition tracks environment. Mass effects: heterogeneous patches, high dispersal, source–sink spillover. Neutral: equivalent species, drift and dispersal limitation.

What does θ measure?

θ = 2JMν, the product of metacommunity size and speciation rate. It sets regional richness and evenness under neutrality and equals Fisher’s α of the log-series.
